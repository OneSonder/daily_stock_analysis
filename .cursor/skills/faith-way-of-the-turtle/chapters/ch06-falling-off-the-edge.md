# Chapter 6: Falling Off the Edge

## Core Idea
Not every "edge" is durable. Support/resistance create short-horizon opportunities — and traps — when breakouts fail and when market structure is shaky.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Support and resistance as behavior**: Zones where prior buyers/sellers reappear due to anchoring, regret, and herd memory.
- **Finding edge in S/R**: Countertrend fades of failed breakouts can have positive short-term E-ratio even when trend breakouts have medium-term edge.
- **Shaky ground warning**: Edges that depend on unstable microstructure, thin liquidity, or regime shifts can vanish.

## Key Concepts
- **Breakout fade**: Prices pierce highs/lows then reverse — food for countertrend methods.
- **Market memory**: Prior extremes become psychological reference points.
- **Edge decay**: What worked in one microstructure/regime may fail after participation changes.
- **False confidence from charts**: Clean S/R pictures can overstate reliability (small samples, visual overfitting).

## Mental Models
- Use **horizon-specific edges**: short-term fade ≠ long-term trend continuation.
- Ask "what behavior creates this S/R?" before trading the lines.
- Treat fragile edges as temporary until stress-tested across regimes.

## Anti-patterns
- Trading every touch of a drawn line as if geometry equals expectancy.
- Applying countertrend logic into strong trending regimes without filters.
- Confusing a few successful fades with a validated edge (law of small numbers).

## Worked Example
Chapter 5 showed breakouts often have short-term adverse excursion (E-ratio < 1 near entry) — the mechanical basis for fading breakouts near S/R. Chapter 6 develops how those failed extensions around support/resistance become the countertrend opportunity — and when that opportunity sits on "shaky ground."

## Key Takeaways
1. S/R edges are mostly psychology made visible in price.
2. Short-term and medium-term edges can oppose each other.
3. Validate fades with the same rigor as trend entries.
4. Beware edges that only work in one market state.
5. Fragility is a first-class risk, not a footnote.
