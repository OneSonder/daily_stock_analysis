# Chapter 8: Risk and Money Management

## Core Idea
Money management is staying alive. Positive expectancy only compounds if you survive drawdowns and shocks. Excessive risk forces quit via psychological blowup or overnight wipeout.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Stay-in-the-game principle**: Primary goal is continuity; time is your ally with positive expectancy.
- **Too much vs too little risk trade-off**: Blow up vs leave money on the table.
- **Conservative early years**: 20–30% compounded beats aggressive 100–200% attempts that reset to zero.
- **N / ATR position sizing**: Size so each market's daily volatility maps to comparable dollar risk.
- **Unit limits (portfolio risk caps)**: Cap units by market, correlated group, and direction.

## Key Concepts
- **Risk of ruin (practical)**: Usually from no plan, excessive leverage, unrealistic expectations — not pure random streaks.
- **N**: 20-day ATR-like volatility measure used by Turtles for sizing.
- **Unit**: Position chunk where 1 N ≈ 1% of equity (Turtle aggressiveness).
- **Volatility normalization**: Equalize dollar swings across markets so diversification works.
- **Stop distance ≠ shock exposure**: Gaps through stops (Oct 1987) make volatility sizing essential.
- **Doctors/dentists trap**: High intelligence + success elsewhere ≠ instant trading competence.

## Mental Models
- Prefer historical sim DD ≤ ~½ psychological max.
- Size for **portfolio correlation on bad days** (everything moves against you together).
- Use unit limits as a filter that keeps you in leading markets and out of lagging ones.

## Anti-patterns
- Trading NG (or any huge ATR market) with tiny accounts at "allowed" margin levels.
- One-contract-per-$X rules that ignore changing volatility.
- Chasing 100%+ returns without planning for unprecedented disasters.
- Believing complex money-management formulas replace judgment about personal risk limits.

## Worked Example
1987 interest-rate reversal: aggressive 3%/trade risk could bust overnight. Unit limits (4/market, 6 correlated, 10–12 direction) likely saved Dennis enormous capital when nearly every position moved against Turtles. Faith notes his tighter stops did not explode size because sizing was volatility-based, not stop-distance-based.

## Key Takeaways
1. Survival first; compounding second.
2. Normalize position size by volatility (N).
3. Cap aggregate risk with unit limits.
4. Unrealistic return targets cause fatal leverage.
5. Plan for worse-than-history shocks.
